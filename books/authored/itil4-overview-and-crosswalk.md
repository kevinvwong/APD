## What ITIL Is

ITIL is a framework for **IT service management (ITSM)** — the discipline of
planning, delivering, operating, and improving the IT services an organization
provides to its users and customers. Where earlier versions framed service
management as a set of sequential processes, ITIL 4 reframes it around _value
co-creation_: an organization and its consumers create value together through
services, and the framework's job is to organize the work that makes that
possible.

ITIL 4 is deliberately not prescriptive. It offers a model and a vocabulary, then
expects each organization to adapt them to its own context rather than following
a fixed procedure. That posture — a shared conceptual frame applied with judgment
to local conditions — is why ITIL sits comfortably alongside doctrine: both are
bodies of professional thought meant to inform decisions, not replace them.

## The Service Value System (SVS)

The **Service Value System** is ITIL 4's top-level model. It describes how the
parts of an organization work together to turn _demand_ and _opportunity_ into
_value_. Its components are:

- **Guiding principles** — durable recommendations that hold in almost any
  situation (see below).
- **Governance** — how the organization is directed and controlled; how
  authority, evaluation, and policy flow.
- **Service value chain** — the operating model: the set of activities that
  convert demand into valuable services (see below).
- **Practices** — the organizational resources and capabilities (people,
  process, tools) brought to bear on the work.
- **Continual improvement** — a recurring activity, woven through everything,
  that keeps the system aligned with changing needs.

The SVS is intentionally a _system_: the components reinforce one another, and a
change in one ripples through the others. This whole-system view is the same
instinct behind treating an organization as an interdependent set of warfighting
functions or lines of effort rather than a stack of isolated departments.

## The Four Dimensions of Service Management

ITIL 4 insists that every service be considered from four angles at once, so that
no aspect of delivery is neglected:

1. **Organizations and people** — structure, roles, culture, and the competence
   of the people doing the work.
2. **Information and technology** — the data, knowledge, and tools the service
   depends on.
3. **Partners and suppliers** — the external relationships and contracts that
   contribute to the service.
4. **Value streams and processes** — how the activities are organized and
   sequenced to deliver outcomes.

Each dimension is shaped by external factors — political, economic, social,
technological, legal, and environmental. The four dimensions are a balance check:
optimize one (say, technology) while ignoring another (say, people) and the
service suffers.

## The Seven Guiding Principles

The guiding principles are ITIL 4's portable advice — the part most worth
internalizing because it transfers far beyond IT:

1. **Focus on value.** Every activity should trace back to value for a
   stakeholder. Work that creates no value is waste.
2. **Start where you are.** Assess the current state honestly before rebuilding;
   do not discard what already works.
3. **Progress iteratively with feedback.** Break work into manageable pieces,
   deliver, learn, and adjust — rather than attempting one large change.
4. **Collaborate and promote visibility.** Involve the right people and make work
   transparent; hidden work and silos breed poor decisions.
5. **Think and work holistically.** No component stands alone; consider the whole
   system and its interactions.
6. **Keep it simple and practical.** Use the minimum steps needed; eliminate
   anything that fails to produce a useful outcome.
7. **Optimize and automate.** Make work as effective as possible first, then use
   technology to handle what should not require human intervention.

## The Service Value Chain

The **service value chain** is the operating model at the center of the SVS. It
is a flexible set of six interconnected activities — not a rigid sequence — that
an organization combines into _value streams_ to respond to demand and deliver
products and services:

- **Plan** — set shared direction, policies, and priorities.
- **Improve** — continually better the products, services, and practices.
- **Engage** — understand stakeholder needs and maintain relationships.
- **Design and transition** — ensure services meet expectations and are released
  into use.
- **Obtain/build** — acquire or create the components services need.
- **Deliver and support** — operate services and handle ongoing user needs.

Because the activities are recombined per value stream rather than run in fixed
order, the value chain behaves more like a repertoire of functions than a
checklist — closer to mission-type tactics than to a fixed drill.

## The 34 Management Practices

ITIL 4 organizes capabilities into **34 management practices** across three
categories. (A _practice_ is a set of organizational resources — people, process,
information, and tools — designed for performing work or accomplishing an
objective.) The practices are listed here by name only, for orientation; the
official guidance defines each in depth.

### General management practices (14)

Drawn from general business management and applied to service management:
Architecture Management, Continual Improvement, Information Security Management,
Knowledge Management, Measurement and Reporting, Organizational Change
Management, Portfolio Management, Project Management, Relationship Management,
Risk Management, Service Financial Management, Strategy Management, Supplier
Management, and Workforce and Talent Management.

### Service management practices (17)

Developed specifically within service management: Availability Management,
Business Analysis, Capacity and Performance Management, Change Enablement,
Incident Management, IT Asset Management, Monitoring and Event Management,
Problem Management, Release Management, Service Catalog Management, Service
Configuration Management, Service Continuity Management, Service Design, Service
Desk, Service Level Management, Service Request Management, and Service Validation
and Testing.

### Technical management practices (3)

Adapted from technology management for service-management purposes: Deployment
Management, Infrastructure and Platform Management, and Software Development and
Management.

## Crosswalk: ITIL and the Doctrine Library

ITIL's vocabulary often names, in service-management terms, ideas this library
already covers in leadership, organizational, and doctrinal terms. The mappings
below are interpretive bridges — an aid to seeing the same idea across
disciplines — not claims that the concepts are identical.

### Guiding principles ↔ leadership and planning doctrine

- **Focus on value** parallels mission command's emphasis on _purpose_ and the
  commander's intent: subordinate every action to the outcome that matters, not
  to activity for its own sake.
- **Start where you are** echoes the running estimate and honest assessment of
  the current situation before committing to a course of action.
- **Progress iteratively with feedback** mirrors the plan–prepare–execute–assess
  cycle and the broader idea of operations as continuous learning.
- **Collaborate and promote visibility** restates shared understanding and the
  common operating picture — decisions improve when the relevant actors see the
  same reality.
- **Think and work holistically** is the systems view behind the warfighting
  functions and lines of effort: parts are understood through their interactions.
- **Keep it simple and practical** is the principle of simplicity directly —
  clear, uncomplicated plans others can execute under stress.
- **Optimize and automate** tracks the management tradition (Taylor onward) of
  improving the method before mechanizing it.

### The four dimensions ↔ organizational analysis

The four dimensions — people, information/technology, partners/suppliers, and
value streams/processes — are a service-management cousin of the frames used to
analyze any organization: human/structural/political/symbolic lenses in
organizational theory, or the personnel–intelligence–operations–logistics
staffing structure that forces a commander to view a force from several angles at
once. In every case the lesson is the same: optimize one dimension while
neglecting another and the whole degrades.

### The service value chain ↔ mission-type operating models

The value chain's recombinable activities resemble doctrine's preference for
_functions executed with judgment_ over fixed procedure. Just as a value stream
assembles Plan/Engage/Deliver to suit a demand, a unit task-organizes
warfighting functions to suit a mission. Both reject the idea that one rigid
sequence fits every situation.

### Continual improvement ↔ the assessment habit

ITIL's continual improvement — woven through the system rather than bolted on at
the end — is the same instinct as continuous assessment and the after-action
review: improvement is a standing activity, not a phase.

### Where the analogy breaks down

The crosswalk is a teaching aid, and it has limits. ITIL is built for _recurring,
consumer-facing service delivery_ under commercial and contractual constraints;
doctrine is built for _operations under adversarial conditions_ where an opponent
actively contests outcomes. ITIL has no real equivalent to a thinking enemy, and
doctrine has no real equivalent to a service-level agreement. Use the mappings to
build intuition across domains — not to substitute one body of guidance for the
other.
